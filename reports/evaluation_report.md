# Evaluation report: hallucination-claim-grounding-eval

This report describes the run committed in `results/results.json`
(regenerated by `run_evaluation.py`, which is deterministic and writes no
date). All data is synthetic; see README.md for the disclosure.

## Setup

21 claims across 18 synthetic fixture cases in `fixtures/corpus.json`,
covering six categories (directly supported, paraphrased-but-supported,
negation-contradicted, numeric-contradicted, unsupported, and compound
answers mixing two claim categories). Each claim's ground-truth label is
stored directly with its fixture, not derived from the classifier's own
output.

## Headline numbers

| Category | Correct / Total | Accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| SUPPORTED | 7 / 9 | 0.778 |
| CONTRADICTED | 8 / 8 | 1.000 |
| UNSUPPORTED | 4 / 4 | 1.000 |
| Overall | 19 / 21 | 0.905 |

Confusion matrix (rows = ground truth, columns = predicted):

| | SUPPORTED | CONTRADICTED | UNSUPPORTED |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SUPPORTED** | 7 | 0 | 2 |
| **CONTRADICTED** | 0 | 8 | 0 |
| **UNSUPPORTED** | 0 | 0 | 4 |

The only errors are two SUPPORTED claims predicted as UNSUPPORTED. No
contradicted or unsupported claim in this corpus was called SUPPORTED.

That result is limited by how the corpus was built. The perfect CONTRADICTED
and UNSUPPORTED rows are results on a small, purpose-built corpus, not
evidence of general robustness. The contradicted and unsupported claims are
direct, and the corpus has no broad coverage of near-miss claims such as
role swaps, antonym substitutions or one-content-word substitutions. A
claim like that shares most of its words with the source, so a lexical
overlap rule can label it SUPPORTED. The corpus does not test that, so
the zero in the SUPPORTED column says nothing about it. The two error
types are also not weighted: this report does not measure which direction
would cost more in a real use.

## The finding

Both misses are paraphrase cases, and both fail for the same reason: the
checker's support rule is word-overlap based, with no synonym or stemming
awareness, and the paraphrased answer replaced enough of the source's
vocabulary that overlap dropped below the 0.6 threshold used to call
something supported.

- `c05_paraphrase_commute`. Source: "cut the average commute ... in
  half." Answer: "dramatically reduced how long it takes ... to reach
  downtown." Measured overlap: 0.4545.
- `c06_paraphrase_solar`. Source: "runs entirely on solar power
  collected from panels on the factory roof." Answer: "draws all of its
  electricity from rooftop solar cells above the plant." Measured
  overlap: 0.3333.

A third paraphrase case, `c04_paraphrase_temperature`, was classified
correctly (overlap 0.8889), but it retained much of the source's
original phrasing ("coldest morning of the year," "temperature,"
"negative 14 degrees"), so it isn't really evidence the checker handles
paraphrase in general. It's evidence that this specific paraphrase didn't
change enough words to matter.

Every negation-contradiction and numeric-contradiction case in the corpus
was caught. Both contradiction rules only fire when the claim shares at
least half of its content words (0.5) with the best-matching source clause,
and every corpus contradiction overlaps by at least 0.8, so the corpus
cannot show what that gate contributes; `tests/test_thresholds.py` covers
the boundary. The caught cases include two compound-answer cases (`c16`, `c17`) where the
source states two facts in one sentence and only one of the two derived
claims is wrong. The checker's clause-level best-match logic (not
whole-document matching) is what keeps the negation/numeric checks
localized to the right part of the source instead of firing on unrelated
text. `tests/test_checker.py::test_negation_localized_to_matching_clause_not_whole_source`
is a regression test for exactly this: a straightforward
whole-sentence-comparison version of this check would produce a false
contradiction on `c17`'s first claim.

## What this does and doesn't support

This result supports a narrow claim: a purely lexical/rule-based claim
checker, on this 21-claim synthetic corpus, handles the direct support,
negation and numeric-mismatch cases in the corpus, and fails on the two
paraphrase cases that share little vocabulary with their source. It does
not support a claim about hallucination detection rates in general, real
model outputs, difficult near-miss claims, or any corpus other than this
one. The checker stays lexical and narrow. 21 claims from a single
synthetic corpus is a demonstration of a mechanism and its failure mode,
not a statistically meaningful sample.

## Input formats

Curly apostrophes and thousands separators are normalized before the
rules run, so "doesn’t" reads like "doesn't" and "4,200" like "4200".
These are not handled: number words ("two", "three"), hyphenated numeric
ranges ("10-14" is read as 10 and negative 14), and textual negatives
("minus 14" is read as a plain 14). The comma rule is meant for standard
thousands separators only: decimal-comma notation ("3,141" is read as 3141)
and malformed grouping ("1,234,56" is read as 1234 and 56) are not
specially supported.

## Ways to make this a stronger benchmark

- Unit-equivalent numeric cases (e.g. "42 inches" vs. "3.5 feet"). The
  current numeric check compares digit strings directly and would treat
  these as simply unrelated numbers rather than recognizing the
  equivalence or flagging a real mismatch.
- A small, explicit, curated synonym list, to see how much of the
  paraphrase gap that alone would close versus how much requires real
  semantic matching.
- Near-miss claims: role swaps, antonym substitutions and single-content-word
  substitutions, with their own category and counts, to measure the
  false-SUPPORTED behaviour this corpus does not test.
- Negation fixtures in both directions (claim negated, source affirmative).
- Claims with more than one sentence, and source documents longer than a
  few sentences, to see whether the clause-level best-match approach
  still finds the right evidence when there's more text to search
  through.
